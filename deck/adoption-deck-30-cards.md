# The Adoption Deck — 30 cards

Thirty cards for partners and principals asking how their firm should adopt AI.
Five strategic questions, one section each: **Can we** (6), **Should we** (6),
**How** (5), **Are we ready** (5), **What's the value** (6), plus an opener and
the read-out.

Two card types:

- **Audit cards** — why it gets dodged, what to write down, the line.
- **Move cards** — why it goes wrong, the move, the line.

Work one section at a time, pen in hand. There is no score at the end; there
are four states, and each points at different work.

---

### 00 · Write, don't think
**Why it gets dodged:** Reflection feels done when it's been thought about. Thought answers hide gaps; written answers expose them.

**The move:** Thirty to sixty minutes, pen in hand, one answer per card, in the firm's own words. Alone first, then with the partners.

*"Written answers expose gaps that mental ones do not."*

---

## CAN WE · Capability and current state

### 01 · The tool list
**Why it gets dodged:** Personal accounts feel invisible, so the firm's real tool count is higher than the paid one — and the invisible tools are the unmanaged ones.

**Write this down:** Every AI tool the firm currently pays for, by name. Plus the personal accounts staff use for work. If you cannot list these confidently, write that down instead.

*"If you cannot list them, that is the answer."*

### 02 · Three tools, named uses
**Why it gets dodged:** "People use it sometimes" feels like an answer. It names neither the work nor the frequency, so it can be neither managed nor improved.

**Write this down:** Take three tools. For each: who uses it, how often, and what for. Specific: "Sarah uses ChatGPT to redraft client replies, two or three times a week."

*"A use you can name is a use you can govern."*

### 03 · The three groups
**Why it gets dodged:** It's easier to sort staff into "uses it" and "doesn't" and stop there. The third group — usually the biggest — is where the unexamined risk sits.

**Write this down:** The staff you'd call habitual, capable AI users, by name. The staff who don't use AI at all. If both groups are smaller than headcount: what is the third group doing?

*"Count the middle group. It's the biggest one."*

### 04 · The unseen use
**Why it gets dodged:** Nobody wants to write down where they think the firm is exposed. But shadow use that goes unnamed also goes ungoverned.

**Write this down:** Where you think AI is being used in the firm that you do not currently see. Almost every firm has an answer.

*"Almost every firm has an answer. The answer matters."*

### 05 · Sustained or tolerated?
**Why it gets dodged:** Firms conflate tolerating AI use with supporting it. Tolerated use depends on individual initiative and dies with it.

**Write this down:** Could the firm sustain AI use it considered important — training, guidance, an internal owner? Or would such use depend on individual initiative?

*"Supported use survives the person. Tolerated use doesn't."*

### 06 · Name the internal owner `move`
**Why it goes wrong:** When nobody owns the question "is this appropriate?", every staff member answers it alone, and the firm's position is whatever the boldest person decided.

**The move:** Name the one person staff would naturally go to with "is this appropriate?" — then tell the firm you named them.

*"A question with no owner gets answered in private."*

## SHOULD WE · Opportunity and risk

### 07 · Where value is plausible
**Why it gets dodged:** "Drafting" is where the thinking stops. Generic answers produce generic adoption, and generic adoption produces nothing defensible.

**Write this down:** Where would AI most plausibly add value if used well? Be specific: not "drafting" but "first-pass drafts of standard property contract clauses where the senior associate's review remains the binding step."

*"Not 'drafting'. Which drafts, reviewed by whom."*

### 08 · Where risk exceeds value
**Why it gets dodged:** "Data leakage" is abstract, so it gets filed under IT and forgotten. The real risks are concrete and sit in named workflows.

**Write this down:** Where would AI introduce risks that exceed the value? Same specificity: "the property associates pasting client emails into ChatGPT to draft replies."

*"Name the workflow, not the category."*

### 09 · The sensitive material
**Why it gets dodged:** Week by week, sensitive material passes through tools without anyone tracking it, because tracking was never anyone's job.

**Write this down:** The most sensitive material your team handles in a week. A month. A year. For each: can you say with confidence whether it has, or has not, passed through any AI tool?

*"'I don't know' is a finding. Write it down."*

### 10 · The regulator question
**Why it gets dodged:** The answer exists in pieces across partners' heads. It has never been written in one place, so it has never been tested.

**Write this down:** Your regulator asks tomorrow how the firm uses AI. The answer, in plain English, three or four sentences. Read it back. Would it satisfy you, in their position?

*"Write the answer before you need it."*

### 11 · The client conversation
**Why it gets dodged:** Clients raising AI feels hypothetical until it arrives, and the first conversation should not be improvised.

**Write this down:** Has any client raised AI — asking you to use it, not to use it, or how you handle their material? What did you tell them? If not: do you expect it within twelve months?

*"The first client to ask deserves a rehearsed answer."*

### 12 · One page, one position `move`
**Why it goes wrong:** The firm's position stays verbal, so it shifts with whoever is asked — and the written form arrives only after an incident.

**The move:** Take the regulator answer and make it the firm's one-page AI position: what staff may do, what needs asking first, who owns the question. Date it. Review twice a year.

*"If it isn't written, it isn't a position."*

## HOW · Implementation orientation

### 13 · Training that changed something
**Why it gets dodged:** Training gets remembered as an event, not measured as a change. If nothing changed afterwards, the training was entertainment.

**Write this down:** Any AI training the firm has tried, formal or informal. Who attended? What changed afterwards?

*"What changed afterwards is the only part that counts."*

### 14 · The policy that exists
**Why it gets dodged:** A one-page memo from two years ago feels like a policy. If staff can't say what it says, it's a document, not a policy.

**Write this down:** Any AI use policy or internal guideline, even one page. Where does it live, when was it last updated, and what does staff actually do in light of it?

*"A policy nobody operates is a wish with a date on it."*

### 15 · The first move
**Why it gets dodged:** Every option argues for itself — workshop, policy, infrastructure, hire. Without naming one, the firm stays in "we should think about this".

**Write this down:** If the firm decided this week to take AI seriously, what would the first move be? Why that one, and not the others?

*"'We should think about this' is not a move."*

### 16 · Where it stalled
**Why it gets dodged:** Stalls get attributed to the last visible excuse. The actual blocker is usually a person or a permission nobody wants to name.

**Write this down:** Where efforts to address this have stalled, and the actual blocker: partner buy-in, IT capacity, competing priorities, lack of clarity, lack of internal authority, something else.

*"Name the blocker, or the blocker wins."*

### 17 · One move, one owner, one date `move`
**Why it goes wrong:** Firms adopt AI as a mood, not a project. Without one named move, nothing has a start date, so nothing starts.

**The move:** From the audit, pick the single highest-leverage first move. One owner, one date, thirty days to evidence.

*"One move with a date beats five moves in a memo."*

## ARE WE READY · Readiness check

### 18 · Where the knowledge lives
**Why it gets dodged:** Firms run for years without a map of where their written knowledge lives, because everyone who needs a document asks the person who has it.

**Write this down:** Where the firm's important written knowledge lives — document management, shared drives, email, individuals' inboxes, paper. Is it organised, searchable, and accessible to the staff who need it?

*"If it lives in one person's inbox, it lives at their pleasure."*

### 19 · The integration map
**Why it gets dodged:** Practice systems get chosen for billing, then quietly become the ceiling on everything else. Nobody asks about integrations until something needs one.

**Write this down:** The technology AI tools would need to integrate with — practice management, document management, accounting, billing — and any of them locked or constrained in ways that would limit it.

*"The system you can't integrate with sets your ceiling."*

### 20 · The local option
**Why it gets dodged:** Sovereign hosting sounds like an enterprise question. For regulated firms it is the question — and "we'd ask someone" is not a plan.

**Write this down:** If the firm wanted sensitive matters in AI workflows without data leaving the building: is there anyone, internally or in its trusted network, who could evaluate or implement local AI infrastructure? If not, what's the plan?

*"'We'd ask someone' is not a plan."*

### 21 · The capacity check
**Why it gets dodged:** Change capacity is assumed to exist because the change feels urgent. Teams already at capacity absorb nothing, and the initiative quietly dies.

**Write this down:** Whether the firm has the people-time to absorb a meaningful change in how it works with AI in the next six to twelve months — or whether the team is already at capacity for any new initiative.

*"No spare hours, no adoption. Count them first."*

### 22 · Ask the lock question `move`
**Why it goes wrong:** Vendors answer integration questions with brochures, and firms discover the constraint after signing, when the lock is already paid for.

**The move:** Ask each critical vendor three questions: can other systems read our data, can they write, and what does that cost? The answers go in the audit file.

*"Ask before the quote, not after the demo."*

## WHAT'S THE VALUE · Business case framing

### 23 · Concrete change
**Why it gets dodged:** "Efficiency" is the shape value takes in press releases. In firms it arrives as specific hours on specific documents.

**Write this down:** If the firm closed its AI literacy gap in the next six months, what changes in concrete terms? Time saved on what; quality improved on what; risk reduced on what?

*"On what?" is the whole question.*

### 24 · The policy's value
**Why it gets dodged:** Policies get valued as compliance objects, so their business case reads weak. The value is in what the firm can finally say and do.

**Write this down:** If the firm wrote and operated a real AI use policy in the next twelve months, what would be the value? Be specific about the value, not the policy itself.

*"Sell the value, not the document."*

### 25 · The client questionnaire
**Why it gets dodged:** The questionnaire is hypothetical until it isn't — and by then, the answer is worth a tender, not a paragraph.

**Write this down:** If a client questionnaire arrived next month asking how the firm handles AI in client material: what is the current answer worth? What would a confident answer be worth?

*"The answer has a price. So does the pause before it."*

### 26 · The cost of inaction
**Why it gets dodged:** Business cases get written for action, because action has a quote attached. Inaction feels free, so its cost is never written.

**Write this down:** What it would cost the firm, in time and money, not to act on this in the next twelve months.

*"The right business case is the cost of inaction."*

### 27 · The honest range
**Why it gets dodged:** Partners hold different invisible numbers. Until they're said out loud, every proposal lands as a surprise.

**Write this down:** What investment range the firm would consider if the case were clear. Tens of thousands? Six figures? Be honest about what the firm would and would not approve.

*"An unspoken range vetoes every plan."*

### 28 · The cost-of-inaction memo `move`
**Why it goes wrong:** AI proposals get judged against other spending, while standing still gets judged against nothing.

**The move:** Write the one-page cost of not acting, in the firm's own terms, and put it beside the investment range before any decision is made.

*"Standing still has an invoice too."*

---

### 29 · What your answers tell you
**Why it goes wrong:** Most firms read diagnostics hoping for a score. There is no score here. There are four states, and each points at different work.

**The read:** Could answer most, confidently and specifically? The work is consolidation — pull the positions into one written framework. Around half vague or generic? You're in the most common state: literacy first, policy second. Most uncertain, several surprises? The firm hasn't formed a position yet; begin with a structured conversation. Wanted to discuss the answers? That signal is the work.

*"No number at the end. The work is in the answering."*
